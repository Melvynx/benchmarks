# Three.js Air Conditioning Understanding

## Purpose

Build a beautiful, interactive 3D teaching experience that makes reversible air conditioning systems genuinely easy to understand.

## The Prompt

---

<role>
You are a senior Three.js engineer, technical illustrator, motion designer, and HVAC educator. You create premium browser-based 3D explainers that combine engineering accuracy, cinematic visual quality, and exceptionally clear interaction design.
</role>

<challenge>
Create a complete, self-contained, single HTML file using Three.js that teaches how reversible air conditioning works.

The scene must use one architectural wall as the central visual divider between an indoor room and the outdoors. Users must be able to switch between:

1. A split-system reversible heat pump with an indoor unit, refrigerant lines passing through the wall, and an outdoor unit.
2. A monoblock reversible air conditioner with the complete refrigeration circuit inside one indoor cabinet and clearly represented outdoor intake and exhaust ducts through the wall.

For both system types, users must also be able to switch between Cooling and Heating modes. The experience should make the differences immediately understandable through animated airflow, refrigerant movement, heat transfer, component behavior, labels, and short guided explanations.
</challenge>

<technical-accuracy>
Teach the real energy flows rather than a misleading decorative story.

- In a split system, indoor and outdoor air do not travel through the refrigerant pipes. The pipes carry refrigerant in a closed loop.
- In Cooling mode, the indoor heat exchanger absorbs heat from room air and the outdoor heat exchanger rejects that heat outside.
- In Heating mode, a reversing valve changes the refrigerant circuit so the outdoor heat exchanger absorbs heat from outdoor air and the indoor heat exchanger releases heat into the room.
- Show the compressor, expansion device, reversing valve, fans, heat exchangers, refrigerant lines, and condensate behavior where relevant.
- For the monoblock system, show both heat exchangers and the refrigeration circuit inside one cabinet. Clearly distinguish indoor recirculated air from the separate outdoor intake/exhaust air path through the wall.
- Make energy conservation intuitive: the system moves heat; it does not create cold.
- Use clear visual distinctions for air particles, refrigerant flow, and heat-energy particles. Include a compact legend.
</technical-accuracy>

<experience>
The final page should feel like a premium interactive museum exhibit or high-end product education tool, not a generic dashboard.

### 3D scene

- Create a polished cutaway environment with a single detailed wall dividing a comfortable interior room from a believable outdoor area.
- Build the indoor split unit, outdoor condenser/heat-pump unit, monoblock cabinet, ducts, pipes, coils, compressor, fans, valves, vents, fittings, and wall penetrations from Three.js geometry. Do not use external 3D models.
- Allow orbiting, gentle zooming, and a reliable cinematic reset view. Constrain the camera so the teaching scene remains legible.
- Use excellent lighting, shadows, depth, materials, glass/cutaway surfaces, subtle bloom or glow where appropriate, and a coherent hot/cold color language.
- Animate fan blades, directional airflow, refrigerant circulation, coil activity, heat absorption/rejection, and condensation. Transitions between system types and operating modes should be smooth and intentional.
- Particle direction, speed, color, and origin must update correctly when Cooling or Heating is selected.
- Keep performance smooth and responsive without sacrificing visual richness.

### Interface

- Show the model name as the primary visible title at the top of the page, with no extra words in that title.
- Provide prominent controls for `Split System` versus `Monoblock`, and `Cooling` versus `Heating`.
- Add Play/Pause, Reset View, and a way to restart the guided explanation.
- Display current indoor and outdoor temperatures, system state, and a concise statement of where heat is being absorbed and rejected.
- Use accessible labels, strong contrast, keyboard-operable controls, visible focus states, and responsive layouts for desktop and mobile.
- Do not hide the 3D scene behind large panels. Keep information compact, layered, and readable.

### Guided tutorial

Create a short, replayable tutorial with small messages that advance through the active system step by step. Each step must focus the camera or highlight the relevant component while the corresponding animation becomes visually prominent.

The tutorial should explain, in this order where applicable:

1. Which air loop is moving and whether that air stays indoors or outdoors.
2. Where heat enters the refrigerant circuit.
3. How the compressor raises refrigerant pressure and temperature.
4. Where heat leaves the refrigerant circuit.
5. How the expansion device prepares the refrigerant to absorb heat again.
6. What the reversing valve changes in Heating mode.
7. The decisive difference between split and monoblock construction.

Keep each message brief, plain-English, and synchronized with the visual action. Include progress, Back, Next, and Close controls. Switching system type or mode should update the tutorial content without stale labels or incorrect arrows.
</experience>

<visual-quality>
Aim for exceptional visual craft:

- A sophisticated, restrained interface with strong hierarchy and refined typography.
- Physically suggestive materials and lighting, soft contact shadows, subtle environmental motion, and attractive composition.
- Beautiful animated particles and paths that remain educational instead of becoming visual noise.
- Clean component labels connected to objects with stable callout lines that avoid overlaps as much as possible.
- Smooth state transitions and polished micro-interactions.
- A convincing cutaway mode that lets users understand internal components without losing the overall system layout.
- A result that looks intentionally art-directed from every default camera position.
</visual-quality>

<technical-constraints>
- Deliver exactly one complete HTML file.
- Load Three.js and any official Three.js helpers from a CDN.
- Put all HTML, CSS, application logic, shaders, procedural textures, and geometry construction in that file.
- Do not use external images, textures, videos, fonts, model files, or APIs.
- Use no build step.
- Handle resize, high-DPI rendering, reduced-motion preferences, and WebGL failure gracefully.
- Avoid fake controls: every visible control must work.
- The page must open directly in a modern browser.
</technical-constraints>

<creative-freedom>
Make the remaining art-direction and implementation decisions yourself. Do not explain the design before building it. Spend the effort on the finished experience, refine it carefully, and prioritize both visual excellence and conceptual clarity.
</creative-freedom>

<success-criteria>
The experience succeeds when a first-time viewer can accurately explain:

- why a split system has two units connected by refrigerant lines;
- why a monoblock contains the full refrigeration circuit in one cabinet and needs separate outdoor airflow paths;
- where heat moves in Cooling mode;
- how and why the direction changes in Heating mode;
- which animated particles represent room air, outdoor air, refrigerant, and heat energy.

The final result must be technically credible, visually impressive, fully interactive, and immediately understandable.
</success-criteria>

<output-format>
Respond with only the complete HTML document, beginning with `<!DOCTYPE html>` and ending with `</html>`. Include all CSS and JavaScript inline. Do not include explanations before or after the HTML.
</output-format>
